## Reel 7 — kit_reel7_braille.mp4 (39 s)
A fifteen-year-old invented a whole alphabet. Then his school banned it. ✋

Louis Braille lost his sight at five after an accident in his father's workshop. At his school in Paris, blind students read raised letters one slow finger at a time. Then an army captain visited with a code of dots meant to be read in the dark. Louis cut it down, and at fifteen he had an alphabet: six dots, read with one fingertip. A new director banned it and burned the books. The students kept using it anyway.

Today the ADA requires braille on every permanent room sign, with the tactile characters mounted 48 to 60 inches above the floor.

Have you ever noticed the braille on a room sign? 👇

## Reel 8 — kit_reel8_tactile.mp4 (43 s)
A man in Japan spent his own money on bumps in the sidewalk. Now they're on every train platform in America. 🟡

Seiichi Miyake's childhood friend was losing his sight and said he could feel the ground change under his shoes. So in 1965 Seiichi designed a block you could read with your feet: dots for caution, bars for go. He paid for it himself. In 1967 the first blocks went down at a crossing by a school for the blind in Okayama. Japan's railways made them standard, then the cities, then the world.

Today the ADA requires a 24-inch strip of truncated domes along every platform edge, each dome 0.2 inches high.

Did you know those yellow bumps had an inventor? 👇

## Reel 9 — kit_reel9_crawl.mp4 (38 s)
She was eight years old, and she left her wheelchair at the bottom of the Capitol steps. 🏛️

March 12, 1990. The Americans with Disabilities Act was stuck in Congress. About a thousand people marched from the White House to the Capitol, and at the steps some got out of their chairs and pulled themselves up, step by step, in front of the cameras. Jennifer Keelan, age 8, climbed with them: "I'll take all night if I have to." That July, the ADA was signed into law.

Today it says at least 60 percent of a building's public entrances must be accessible. Steps can't be the only way in.

Had you heard of the Capitol Crawl? 👇

## Reel 10 — kit_reel10_dpn.mp4 (43 s)
A university for the deaf had never had a deaf president. In 1988, its students shut the whole school down. ✊

Gallaudet University, Washington, D.C. The board chose the only hearing candidate over two deaf finalists, and the board's chair reportedly said deaf people were "not able to function in a hearing world." Students closed the gates with four demands, starting with a deaf president, now. Faculty and alumni joined. After eight days the board gave in, and I. King Jordan became Gallaudet's first deaf president.

Two years later came the ADA. Today it requires fire alarms in public spaces to flash, not just sound.

Have you seen a strobe alarm go off? 👇

## Reel 11 — kit_reel11_dart.mp4 (44 s)
One man in a wheelchair visited all fifty states to help write a single law. 🇺🇸

Justin Dart got polio at eighteen and was given three days to live. In 1981 he was appointed to the National Council on Disability to help write a national policy on equal rights. When Washington wanted proof that discrimination was real, Justin and his wife Yoshiko went to find it: public forums in every state, more than 30,000 people telling their stories, and the records delivered to Congress. On July 26, 1990, the ADA was signed with Justin on the stage.

Today it requires an accessible route in every new public building, from the parking space to the front door.

What's the first barrier you hit at a building? 👇
